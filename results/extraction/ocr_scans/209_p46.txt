The results also suggest that opening new interstate highways will not increase net economic
activity in non-metropolitan regions: This is due to two effects. First, economic activity appears
to move away from adjacent counties towards highway counties. Second, the intra-county
reallocation of industrial structure led to a rearrangement, but no increase, in economic activity.
“This result, in conjunction with the fact that interstate highway construction estimates range
from $18.04 to $20.27 million per mile, questions the logic of viewing highways as the panacea
for growth in non-metropolitan America®.”

The data and findings in these studies suggest that local economic development success is related
to the degree of access and connectivity improvement that the highway investment provides. It
also depends on the type and strength of non-highway economic development initiatives in the
area. Several studies indicate that the beneficiaries of interstate links in terms of economic
growth are most often counties in close proximity to large cities or counties that have some
degree of prior urbanization. Other studies found that non-metropolitan regions did not
experience increased net economic activity due to a new interstate highway.

These general findings, and the understanding that local economic development often represents
a transfer of activity from another region and nat new economic activity for the state, reduce the
importance of economic development when determining the benefits of a freeway investment
between Watertown and Plattsburgh. An accepted approach to determining whether the benefits
generated by a project exceed the costs is Benefit-Cost Analysis, which measures public benefits
generated by a transportation investment. This approach was utilized as part of this overall
analysis and is discussed below.

2. Benefit-Cost Analysis Framework

BCA is a conceptual framework that calculates and compares the benefits and costs of a project.
It is considered an industry standard for major transportation infrastructure projects, and it serves
two purposes. First, it provides a measure of project feasibility. Second, it offers a basis for
comparing two or more alternatives within a single project.

Within the BCA framework, benefits are broadly defined. They represent the extent to which
people affected by the project are made better off, as measured by their own willingness to pay.
In other words, central to BCA is the idea that people are best able to judge what is good for
them and to identify what improves their well-being or welfare.

Costs incorporated in a BCA generally include capital costs, right-of-way costs, and operating
and maintenance costs (O&M) during the analysis period.
