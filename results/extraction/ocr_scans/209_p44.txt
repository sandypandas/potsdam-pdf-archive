1. Rural Interstates and Economic Development

Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) and others have conducted studies that examine the
economic development impact of rural roadways. A discussion of findings from these studies is
provided below.

FHWA

In 2005, FHWA directed research that focused on the economic development history of
completed interstates or long portions of such interstates (e.g., I-43, I-81) similar to those
designated by Congress (e.g., 1-69, I-73/74). The intent of the research was to gain some
perspective with which to anticipate what would happen with completion of future interstates.

The FHWA research found that in a number of counties, economic growth was feeble despite
proximity to an interstate highway. An example was a case examined for Emanuel, Treutlen,
Wilkinson and Twiggs counties in the I-16 corridor of Georgia. For these counties, interstate
advantages such as employer access to labor, labor mobility, and supplier market access, may
have been less significant than other economic conditions, such as the relative decline of the U.S.
industrial economy. According to the study, there is some speculation that economic activity in
some portions of these counties was actually suppressed by I-16. It facilitated retail activity
outside the area that would have remained in the county, it diverted through traffic from non-
interstate highways, and it acted as a physical barrier to commerce.

On the other hand, there were a number of positive experiences in other corridors. In the I-16
corridor, Bryan County’s jobs increased by approximately 420 percent and its population by 290
percent between 1969 and 2002. This county had a 2002 population of 25,000 and is located
within the Savannah Metropolitan Statistical Area. In comparison, Georgia experienced a 130
percent increase in jobs and a 90 percent increase in population over this same time period.
